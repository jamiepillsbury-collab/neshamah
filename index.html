Why Neshamah

Breath. God breathed life into the first human, and Hebrew's three words for soul are all breath. Breath works on many levels here: slow breathing for the body, prayer and song carried on the breath, writing as the breath of life on the page, sleep as handing the neshamah back to God for the night, and grounding as coming back to nefesh and ruach, being alive and present.

The tea. When my rabbi's wife is sick, he makes her tea. Science can explain how the water heated: the flame, the energy, the vapor. But he only put the kettle on because he loves her. Without the flame, or without the love, that water would not be hot. Both are true, and neither cancels the other. Is it prayer or science that helps the pain? Yes.

People of the Book. Writing, speaking, and singing: journaling, prayer, and song.

Why "Neshamah"?

Torah says God blew the breath of life into the first human, and Hebrew has three words for soul, all of them breath: nefesh (the living self), ruach (the wind moving through you), and neshamah (the breath-soul, entrusted into God's hand at night and returned each morning).

Genesis 2:7 · The Torah: A Modern Commentary, rev. ed. (Plaut)

For pain

Even Rabbi Yochanan, a healer, needed someone to hold out a hand when he fell ill: "a prisoner cannot free himself" (Berakhot 5b). These are hands to hold. Your care team's plan comes first.

How: a slow-exhale body scan, from MBSR, which has good research support for chronic pain. Why: the Zohar maps the ten sefirot onto the body: your body as a map of the Divine.

How: meeting pain with curiosity and safety instead of alarm, from Pain Reprocessing Therapy. Best for pain your doctors don't link to tissue damage, so ask your team whether it fits you.

How: moving attention between a place that feels okay and the edge of the pain, from Somatic Experiencing. You choose how close to go, every time.

About the sefirot map: what's old, what's new

Old and Jewish: the body map comes from Tikkunei Zohar (Castile, c. 1300), in the passage known as Patach Eliyahu, still recited in Sephardic and Hasidic communities: "Chesed is the right arm, Gevurah the left arm, Tiferet the torso, Netzach and Hod the two legs, Yesod the end of the body, Malchut the mouth… Chochmah the brain, Binah the heart, and with it the heart understands." Its root is Job 19:26, "from my flesh I will see God." Breath as a path to God goes back to Abraham Abulafia (13th century), and the Talmud teaches that the Shechinah rests above the head of the sick (Shabbat 12b).

New: moving attention through the body region by region comes from modern mindfulness research. This practice is a bridge: the science answers how, the tradition answers why.

Not this: "Qabalah" with a Q (Golden Dawn, Crowley), sefirot-equals-chakras charts, and Christian Cabala, which used the sefirot to argue for the Trinity and is still recycled on messianic sites.

Tikkunei Zohar 17a (via Sefaria) · Job 19:26 · Shabbat 12b · Berakhot 5b · further reading: Arthur Green, Ehyeh; Tirzah Firestone, Wounds into Wisdom

Brachot — bless what you notice

Something stopped you — a butterfly, a rainbow, the wind? Tap it and its blessing appears. Pause one breath with the moment first.

How: gratitude practice. In studies (Emmons & McCullough, 2003, and later reviews) noticing and naming good moments modestly improves well-being; effects on anxiety and depression are small but real. Lingering a breath on the moment — savoring — helps it land. Why: Rabbi Meir taught saying a hundred blessings a day (Menachot 43b). A bracha turns noticing into relationship.

Hitbodedut — your own words

The Hasidic practice of speaking to God out loud, unscripted, in your own language. The map — three states, plus blended

🌿 Settled (ventral vagal) — emotions can be tolerated, learning possible, can ride the wave.

⚡ Wired (sympathetic) — anxiety, panic, flooding, hypervigilance, pain louder, racing thoughts, can't sleep.

🌗 Both at once (blended freeze) — trapped charge: high arousal and shutdown in the same body. Wired and heavy together.

🌑 Heavy (dorsal vagal) — numb, far away, "I feel nothing," can't think or feel, memory fuzzy.

None of these is a failure. Each one is your system protecting you. The map just helps you choose what helps.

1 · ORIENT — where am I?

Look around and remind yourself: Where am I? What day is it? What time is it? What am I doing right now? What do I see around me? What is different about here and now compared with previous medical experiences?

Be as descriptive as you can, and gently remind yourself: you are here, right now.

2 · GROUND — connect with what's around you

Look

· Name 5 things you can see. · Describe one object in detail. · Find objects of different colors or shapes.

Listen

· Name 3 sounds you can hear. · Notice the closest sound and the farthest sound.

Feel

· Press your feet into the floor. · Notice the chair supporting you. · Press your hands together. · Hold something textured or cool. · Push gently against a wall.

Move

· Take a slow walk. · Stretch. · Roll your shoulders. · Shift weight from one foot to the other.

You do not have to focus intensely on your body. If focusing inward makes you feel more disconnected or anxious, return your attention to the environment around you.

3 · DIFFERENTIATE — what is happening now?

Ask: "Is this something happening right now, something that happened before, or something I'm afraid could happen?"

Then: What do I actually know right now? What am I worried might happen? Is there something I need to do medically — or do I need to regulate while I figure out the next step?

A physical symptom can be real AND my nervous system can also be reacting to what I've experienced before. (This part is confusing, and can really suck.)

You do not have to choose between taking your body seriously and taking care of your nervous system.

4 · CHOOSE — what can I do next?

What is within my control right now? What is the next appropriate step? Do I need to contact my medical provider or follow my existing medical plan? Would grounding, movement, or talking to someone help?

Try: "I don't have to figure out everything right now. I can focus on the next step."

Cold water on the face (dive reflex)

Hard-wired survival reflex: cold on the face drops the heart rate and shifts the body into a mode physiologically incompatible with panic. Not a metaphor — a direct parasympathetic override.

· Fill a bowl with cold water (ice if available), or run cold tap water.

· Submerge your face — forehead, eyes, cheeks — for 15–30 seconds. Or hold a cold cloth firmly over eyes and cheeks.

· Come up, breathe normally, notice the shift.

This is the emergency brake — for when anxiety has escalated to panic and other tools feel inaccessible. Home use, not public. And as always with your chest symptoms: your care team's plan comes first, always.

Slow intentional movement

Anxiety fixes the body — held breath, braced shoulders, rigid jaw. Slow movement reintroduces flow.

· Find where you feel the most tension. Move that area at half the speed you think you should — slower is more regulating.

· Breathe into the sensation. Let the movement spread to neighboring areas — neck, spine, hips.

· After 3–5 minutes, pause and notice the difference between where you started and where you are now.

Grief

Grief isn't only for death. A body, health, work, a future you planned, who you used to be — those losses are real, even when no one sits shiva for them.

How: writing about loss (Pennebaker's expressive writing) helps many people a little — stop if it floods you. Grief comes in waves; turning toward it and turning back toward life are both healthy (the dual process model). Why: Judaism gives mourning a shape and time — shiva, sh'loshim, Kaddish, Yizkor. Mourning is a mitzvah, not a weakness.
